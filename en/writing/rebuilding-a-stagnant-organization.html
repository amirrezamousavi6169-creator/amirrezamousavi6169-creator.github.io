How I rebuilt a stagnant organization

Some organisations aren't failing. They're stagnant — and the fix almost never starts with money.

Some organisations aren't failing. They're stagnant — still operating, still staffed, still producing something, but with no mechanism for getting better. The explanation people reach for inside the building is money. It's almost never money.

A few years ago I worked with a tourism organisation that had reached exactly this state. It was waiting on government funding and didn't have money to buy chairs. The assumed fix was finding one big sponsor.

The sponsor was never the problem. The organisation had no structure for generating revenue at all.

Three flaws, none of them financial

Looking closely, three things were true:

  • Positions existed without function. Roles existed that didn't correspond to any actual work. No one could say what a given role was accountable for, which meant no one was accountable.
  • Every revenue source was external. Government funding, other institutions, one-off support. Not a single revenue line came from the organisation's own activity. This is the deepest kind of fragility: when your income depends entirely on decisions made elsewhere, you don't have a strategy, you have hope.
  • There was no organisational chart. None. Which sounds like an administrative detail and isn't — without one, there's no way to see overlap, no way to assign responsibility, no way to know who does what.

What I built

Four revenue paths, where there had been none:

  • Putting historic buildings to work. The organisation had access to buildings with historic value that were structurally usable. Heritage funding could restore them; the organisation could operate them as cafes, boutique stays and training spaces. Restoration on one budget, revenue on another.
  • A premium layer on the city tours. Defined routes and services for a specific kind of visitor, priced accordingly. That audience already existed and was already being served exactly like everyone else.
  • Sponsorship and advertising. Far easier to set up than assumed, and required no new capability — just someone willing to treat it as a real job.
  • Support for local producers. Independent makers and sellers, given booths, run as a recurring festival rather than a one-off event. It served the organisation's public mission and generated revenue at the same time.

None of these is a contract. Each is a structure that repeatedly produces contracts.

After that came the internal work: an organisational chart, so responsibility could be located. And a layer of oversight — people who already had a job took on the added task of monitoring, so problems surfaced from inside instead of waiting for an annual review.

What I tell anyone in this position

Don't start with money. Start with structure. An organisation that can't say who is accountable for what will waste any budget it receives.

Look for revenue whose raw materials you already own. All four paths above used assets the organisation already had — buildings, an audience, visibility, local relationships. None required a capability it didn't have. The most common blindness in a stagnant organisation is failing to see what it's already sitting on.

Build a mechanism, not an event. A single sponsorship is a transaction. A sponsorship function that renews is a structure. Only the second one changes the trajectory.

Accountability has to be continuous. Oversight that arrives once a year finds problems late — later than they're cheap to fix.

The result, and an honest warning

I went back about a year and a half later. They'd implemented a large part of the roadmap — badly in places, worse than it should have been done — but the organisation had moved.

That part is worth paying attention to. Structure is more forgiving than strategy. A good structure, badly executed, still produces movement, because the mechanism keeps working even when the people running it are imperfect. A brilliant plan with no structure underneath it produces nothing.

If your organisation is stable but stagnant, the fix is usually structural — and structural problems are the kind I spend the most time on.
